Everything Is Tuberculosis

by John Green

Rating: ★★★★★

Summary

Tuberculosis has been entwined with humanity for millennia. Once romanticized as a malady of poets, today tuberculosis is seen as a disease of poverty that walks the trails of injustice and inequity we blazed for it. In 2019, author John Green met Henry Reider, a young tuberculosis patient at Lakka Government Hospital in Sierra Leone. John became fast friends with Henry, a boy with spindly legs and a big, goofy smile. In the years since that first visit to Lakka, Green has become a vocal advocate for increased access to treatment and wider awareness of the healthcare inequities that allow this curable, preventable infectious disease to also be the deadliest, killing over a million people every year. In Everything Is Tuberculosis, John tells Henry’s story, woven through with the scientific and social histories of how tuberculosis has shaped our world—and how our choices will shape the future of tuberculosis.

My Thoughts

What a brilliant novel. If it was not for outside commitments, I would have gotten through this book in one sitting. John Green is an excellent storyteller, and has a brilliant lyricism, that made this book so pleasant to read, even given the historical, cultural, and medical facts thrown in. He has such a way of telling Henry's tale that made me cling to my seat throughout all of the chapters describing his journey through the numerous treatments and hospitals he was admitted to. The links to the important historical events influenced in some way or another by tuberculosis or consumption were clear and easy to comprehend; the cultural impacts on the treatment of the illness throughout the years were extremely insightful to learn about. The medical facts did not overpower the book, and did not feel overwhelming either, but instead they felt like the author takes his readers seriously and trusts in their ability to follow his story which was a nice touch. He didn't overexplain any of the concepts, and instead he took the time to explain them clearly the first time he defined them.

I had no idea that the murder of Archduke Franz Ferdinand was caused by a group of young men afflicted with the disease, who had nothing left to lose, and decided to go out with a bang. Just as I had no idea that the reason that antibiotic resistance in countries like Sierra Leone was mostly because of the exorbitant prices of the treatments imposed by big corporations, as well as the lack of proper nourishment, access to clean water, and improper supply of those treatments that would go out of stock often, and force patients to stop the antibiotics. Henry's story of his father pulling him out of the hospital because of a difference in beliefs was so tragic, and to think that he could have been cured long ago if that had simply not happened and if his father had trusted in science is crazy.

Genuinely a pleasure to read this book, and now I am looking for even more books of this sort to consume because this story was so beautifully written and described.
