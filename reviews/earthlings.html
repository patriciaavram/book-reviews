Earthlings

by Sayaka Murata

Rating: ★★★☆☆

Summary

Natsuki isn't like the other girls. She has a wand and a transformation mirror. She might be a witch, or an alien from another planet. Together with her cousin Yuu, Natsuki spends her summers in the wild mountains of Nagano, dreaming of other worlds. When a terrible sequence of events threatens to part the two children forever, they make a promise: survive, no matter what. Now Natsuki is grown. She lives a quiet life with her asexual husband, surviving as best she can by pretending to be normal. But the demands of Natsuki's family are increasing, her friends wonder why she's still not pregnant, and dark shadows from Natsuki's childhood are pursuing her. Fleeing the suburbs for the mountains of her childhood, Natsuki prepares herself with a reunion with Yuu. Will he still remember their promise? And will he help her keep it?

My Thoughts

Why must life always lead me toward cannibalistic novels?? I genuinely did not expect any of the plot twists in this story to happen, and was mostly pleasantly surprised with the twists and turns of this book, but the finality of the book made me extremely confused. I'm wondering how in the world three sensible and relatively smart people get entangled in this mess. What were they smoking out there in the mountains? Children’s lives never belong to them. The grownups own us. Moreover, I found the commentary on bodily autonomy in a "baby factory" society, as they call it, extremely relevant and interesting. Children, as it seems, exist mostly to fulfill the grownups' desire to fulfill their own duty to society, and not to become their own people that make their own choices. Natsuki being sexually assaulted from a young age obviously had some long-lasting effects on her psyche and on her own body, as she mentions an inability to hear from one of her ears and to taste food as well after the events transpiring between her teacher and herself. There should have definitely been multiple trigger warnings, as the book cover does not give a potential reader the right information on the book's contents. Anyway, this book did bring over some interesting points about how society defines a "right way" to be in a relationship, which is rather about children than about companionship. I respected Natsuki and her husband's relationship a lot, as they had found a way of living together that worked for both of them, and they seemed happy with it. Overall, a very gory book, but bringing forward important questions about humans' accepted behaviours in society and children's place in it.
